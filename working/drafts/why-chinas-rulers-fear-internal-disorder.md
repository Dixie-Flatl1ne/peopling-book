# Why China's rulers fear internal disorder

*Originally written in May 2020. Completed and revised in August 2026.*

When Beijing looked at the protests in Hong Kong, it did not just see people demanding political rights.[7]
It saw the beginning of a familiar sequence: internal disorder, weakened central authority, foreign involvement and national humiliation.

That does not justify what China did in Hong Kong. It does help explain why the Chinese state treated a political protest movement as an existential threat.

I originally described this as four Chinese regimes being destroyed by rebellion followed by invasion. That was too neat. History rarely cooperates with arrows on a whiteboard. The more defensible pattern is that internal breakdown and foreign pressure repeatedly fed each other. Weak government invited rebellion. Rebellion weakened government further. Foreign powers then found a country less able to resist them.

The Communist Party did not merely study this pattern. It was born from it.

## The pattern

In 1644, the Ming dynasty did not simply lose a conventional war to the Manchus.[1]
A rebel army led by Li Zicheng captured Beijing.[1]
A Ming general, Wu Sangui, then asked the Manchus for help defeating Li.[1]
They helped, took Beijing and stayed.[1]
The Qing dynasty emerged from a Chinese rebellion, a failed centre and an outside force invited through the gate.[1]

The Qing faced the same interaction in a more destructive form during the nineteenth century.
Britain and France defeated China in the Opium Wars, forced open ports and imposed treaties which stripped away parts of Chinese sovereignty.[2]
At the same time, the Qing was fighting enormous internal rebellions.[3]
The Taiping Rebellion alone lasted fourteen years, ravaged seventeen provinces and may have killed around twenty million people.[3]
It also forced the Qing to depend on regional armies, weakening central control even after the rebellion was defeated.[3]

Foreign powers did not simply attack a unified China from outside.
They operated inside a country already being pulled apart.
Chinese rebellions were also shaped by foreign ideas and intervention.[3]
Hong Xiuquan, who led the Taiping, believed he was the younger brother of Jesus.[3]
Western-trained forces later helped the Qing defeat him.[3]
The line between rebellion and invasion was never clean.

The Republic of China inherited the wreckage.
It faced warlords, Communist revolt and Japanese invasion at the same time.[4]
Japan's occupation weakened the Nationalist government while the Communist Party built support and military strength in the countryside.[4]
After Japan surrendered, the Soviet Union occupied Manchuria and withdrew once Communist forces were positioned to take control.[4]
The Communists also acquired large stocks of Japanese weapons there.[4]
Corruption and mismanagement had already hollowed out support for Chiang Kai-shek's government.[4]

Once again, the sequence was not simply rebellion followed by invasion. It was worse. Civil conflict, foreign invasion and state failure reinforced one another until the existing government could no longer hold the country together.

## The Party's founding story

The Chinese Communist Party presents itself as the organisation which ended this cycle.

Its official history begins with the Opium War, foreign aggression, unequal treaties, rebellion and the failure of every earlier attempt to save China.[6]
The Party's 2021 historical resolution names the Taiping movement, the Boxer movement and the 1911 Revolution as failed responses to national weakness.[6]
It then presents the Communist victory as the event which restored unity, independence and dignity.[6]

This story does real political work. The Party does not claim legitimacy only because it delivers economic growth. It claims legitimacy because it stopped China from being divided, invaded and humiliated.

That makes stability more than a policy preference.
In the Party's own account, national security and social stability are fundamental tasks.[6]
Challenges to central authority can therefore be interpreted as the first stage of national collapse rather than normal political disagreement.

This is the lens through which Beijing saw Hong Kong.

The 2019 protests included a broad mix of demands and tactics, but Beijing focused on separatism, attacks on state authority and contact with foreign governments.[7]
In 2020 it imposed the National Security Law, criminalising broadly defined acts of secession, subversion, terrorism and collusion with foreign forces.[7]

From outside China, this looked like the destruction of Hong Kong's political freedoms. From inside the Party's historical model, it was the centre closing a breach before internal unrest connected with foreign pressure.

Both descriptions can be true. Explanation is not absolution.

## The weakness inside the solution

The original draft ended just as it reached the most interesting problem.

A system built to prevent disorder can become unable to see reality.

The Great Leap Forward is the clearest example.
Mao demanded impossible increases in agricultural and industrial production.[5]
Local officials had strong incentives to report the success their superiors expected.[5]
Grain yields were exaggerated as the real harvest fell.[5]
The centre continued taking grain from badly affected provinces and exporting grain because the reports told it there was abundance.[5]

This was not simply a collection of dishonest officials. It was a failure produced by the hierarchy itself. Bad news became dangerous to the person reporting it. Each layer made the numbers more acceptable before passing them upwards. By the time the story reached the top, the state was acting against a famine it could not properly see.

The system was designed to concentrate authority so the country could not fragment. The same concentration weakened the information reaching that authority.

That is the paradox. Beijing fears chaos because Chinese history gives it reasons to fear chaos. It answers by tightening control, narrowing acceptable speech and treating independent organisation as a possible security threat. Those measures can suppress open disorder. They can also suppress the information needed to distinguish a real threat from an avoidable policy failure.

A government which assumes dissent is the start of rebellion will rarely hear dissent as useful information. A government which assumes foreign contact is the start of invasion will treat domestic criticism as evidence of an external plot. The historical pattern becomes a template placed over every new event.

Hong Kong was not the Taiping Rebellion. Foreign journalists, diplomats and campaigners were not an invading army. But the Party's political memory compresses the distance between those things. Protest becomes subversion. Subversion becomes foreign interference. Foreign interference becomes another century of humiliation waiting to begin.

The fear is real. So is the danger created by the response.

China's rulers built a state capable of preventing the fragmentation which destroyed earlier regimes. The harder problem is whether that state can hear bad news before it becomes a crisis. History taught Beijing to fear rebellion. It may have taught it too well.
